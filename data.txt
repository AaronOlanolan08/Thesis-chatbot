Abstract
This study proposes an enhanced YOLOv12 architecture with a Dual-Branch Input Stem for underwater object detection. Underwater environments often introduce visual challenges such as noise, blur, color distortion, and low visibility, which reduce the effectiveness of object detection models. To address this problem, the research integrates a dual-branch input design into the baseline YOLOv12 model. One branch preserves structural image features through standard convolutional layers, while the second branch applies depthwise separable convolutions for denoising and noise-resistant feature extraction. The outputs of both branches are combined through adaptive feature fusion to improve detection robustness. The study used merged underwater datasets from DUO and RF100, containing 10,000 annotated images across four object classes: echinus, holothurian, scallop, and starfish. Performance was evaluated using Precision, Recall, mAP@50, and mAP@50:95 under clean and noisy testing conditions. Results showed that the enhanced model consistently outperformed the baseline YOLOv12 model, particularly under moderate and severe noise conditions, with notable improvements in Recall and mAP metrics. These findings indicate that the enhanced architecture improves noise robustness and supports more reliable underwater object detection.

1. INTRODUCTION
1.1 Background Of The Study
Many things can be seen underwater. One example is the marine living features, such as various invertebrates, including corals, seagrass, and fish, which can be observed below artificial objects and pipelines. The problem in underwater detection of objects is noise, which causes the visual quantity to be lost, resulting from the water itself and its surrounding aquatic environment. Images taken below the ocean surface have their limitations, causing the image to suffer from problems such as blurring, color distortion, and loss of essential details, making detection very challenging. These have become an issue because we all know that underwater infrastructure monitoring and marine conservation play a crucial role in protecting the environment [1].

In traditional methods, surveys are conducted through manual diving, which can be hazardous and has several limitations. Detecting these objects is also challenging due to noise, such as color casts and poor visibility [2]. These challenges can lead to serious problems, including habitat damage, pollution, and effects related to climate change, which significantly impact coral reefs, coastal areas, and marine ecosystems.

Issues related to lighting and color consistency persist, despite these enhancements. Studies have focused on enhancing underwater images by improving their contrast and clarity. Color and details. These will help both humans and computer systems better recognize and detect underwater objects [3]. 
Generally, modern object detection methods can be categorized into two main categories. The first group works by first identifying possible regions of interest and classifying them, a process known as two-stage detectors. The second group includes one-stage detectors, such as YOLO (You Only Look Once), which skips the proposal step and predicts object classes and their locations in a single pass. YOLO has become quite popular because it hits a suitable balance between speed and accuracy, making it reasonable for real-world use. Over the years, YOLO models have continued to improve through upgrades in backbone architectures, enhanced multi-scale feature fusion, and more efficient prediction heads. Newer versions also include optimizations that make them more manageable to deploy in field settings, even when computing resources are limited [4]. The YOLO family of networks also has CNN-based detection models for objects and has been found to function well.
The YOLO family has been continually evolving, and the latest, most advanced version is YOLOv12. A2 (Area Attention) and R-ELAN (Residual Efficient Layer Aggregation Networks)  are utilized on this model to enhance and improve the detection performance. It also contributed to boosting the accuracy of detection, while introducing challenges in maintaining robustness against noisy visual elements. YOLOv12 still has a limitation, which is its susceptibility to image noise in underwater images due to poor visibility and color distortion, resulting in low accuracy across all models. YOLOv'12 architecture may be robust, but it still suffers from low performance due to image noise [5][6]. The objects below the water are difficult to detect, which also restricts the effectiveness of these models, suggesting that they should have consistent performance to ensure excellent accuracy on underwater image objects. It is also noted that, although YOLOv12 is the standard due to its latest version, it still has limitations, such as reduced robustness in noisy environments. Solving this problem for enhancing stability in underwater system monitoring that detects objects underwater.
A dual-branch network that handles noise and distorted images is a recent advancement in computer vision. It has two pathways, one for optimizing the noise suppression and the other for cleaning the features of the structure. This allows the model to seize various helpful information that can improve its quality features, even though the images of underwater scenes are noisy. The model removes noise first before undergoing the complicated feature extraction process. It also helps prevent the noise from becoming more pronounced as it blends into the background. A study has also shown that input stage adjustments are very beneficial for improving the stability of models when working with underwater images [7][8]. 
The denoising branch uses the depthwise detachable convolutions. This is lighter and faster in filtering details. This is also very important as it splits the process into two parts: one that filters each channel separately and the other that combines the channels. The afterward will be beneficial in decreasing the handling of operations while preserving the color changes, important edges, and the pattern on the texture, which is a requirement for the accurate detection of underwater objects [7][8][9].
A study demonstrates that it is possible to achieve strong denoising while remaining small and efficient, as noted by Sandler et al. with their lightweight CNN designs [9], which is a very suitable approach in terms of real-world underwater setups where gadgets and devices cannot handle heavy models. The Dual-Path Input Stem's integration will address the YOLOv12's issue of noise underwater by incorporating convolutions of depthwise separable filters, which is also helpful in solving the problem, as it helps reduce the model's massive computational load. This will make the detection of underwater objects easier, more practical, and stable to use.

This study enhances the baseline of the YOLOv12's architecture, which improves the detection performance in underwater noise conditions by integrating and modifying the dual-branch input system. The study's methods follow a specific sequence of measures, including dataset acquisition, preprocessing, model implementation and training, and evaluation.
2.1 Data Acquisition
The datasets used in this study are explicitly utilized only for the detection of underwater objects. The datasets are sourced from Roboflow's DUO [13] dataset and the RF100 dataset [14]. These images have already been annotated with bounding boxes and classified into four categories: echinus, holothurian,  scallop, and starfish. Some of the images in the dataset still include multiple classes within a single image.  
The RF100 and DUO datasets meet all the requirements of YOLOv12's input, which is also obtained from Roboflow. The images in the dataset have a fixed dimension with a uniform size of 640 × 640 pixels. To not cause confusion in the orientations of different cameras, and to ensure they are placed in the correct direction, auto orientation is included, and it is also part of the process. The images are labeled according to their class, and the dataset will be ready for the next step, such as preprocessing and training the model. This will resize of the images, correct their orientation, and add proper labels. During the training process, the labeled images will help guide the model.

2.4 YOLOv12 (Baseline Model)

The object detection models from the YOLO lineage have made a breakthrough with YOLOv12, the latest and most advanced version of that family, yielding more precise detection through feature extraction and multi-scale fusion optimization. Additionally, the attention-driven model is well-suited for detecting small, overlapping, or visually complex objects, as it can direct its attention to the image areas that contain the most informative content.

2.4.1 Principal Components
The architecture of the YOLOv12 model is divided into three parts: the backbone, the neck, and the head.

2.4.1.1 Backbone
YOLOv12 has its backbone built around a sequence of convolutions that process the input image, while also gradually reducing spatial resolution and increasing feature depth. The first layer is a 3x3 convolutional layer with a stride of 2, which extracts basic visual patterns and generates the feature map. The next one, besides downsizing the image even further, adds new channels, which makes the network capable of learning very intricate representations.

While delving deeper into the backbone, YOLOv12 employs multi-scale feature extraction with advanced modules, such as R-ELAN and Area Attention, among others. These modules lay a strong foundation for spatial and contextual understanding by not only enhancing feature representation but also enabling cross-scale fusion. The tiered processing ultimately results in the creation of three major feature maps, P3, P4, and P5, with varying resolutions for the robust detection of both small and large objects [10]. 

2.4.1.2 Neck
In YOLOv12, small, medium, and large object detection is boosted in the neck component of the model. It is formed from the multiple layers of the backbone. It works by taking feature maps from different scales, upsampling them, and then combining them. First, the highest-level feature map (P5) is upsampled using nearest-neighbor interpolation and merged with the mid-level map (P4). The merged result then goes through an R-ELAN module for refinement. This approach is repeated for the low-level feature map (P3). This fusion strategy allows the combination of the spatial information from lower layers with high-level semantic information. This results in richer and more effective feature representations.

After the features are fused, the network performs the final detection step. Downsampling these features brings information from different scales, which creates a stronger object representation. At the largest scale (P5/32-large), R-ELAN is used to maintain high-resolution details while reducing computational cost. The detection layer is then presented with the maps of features that have been enhanced, which are the ones that predict the class and location of objects across all scales [10].


2.4.1.3 Head
The head takes feature maps generated by the neck and transforms them for final predictions. It produces the bounding box coordinates and classification scores. It applies a decoupled architecture that discriminates localization and classification via the use of light-depthwise separable convolutions, leading to the generation of three detection outputs: P3, P4, and P5, which are in charge of small, medium, and large coral detections, respectively, thus guaranteeing precise detection regardless of coral size [7][8].

2.4.2.1 Area Attention Module
The conventional self-attention approach is computationally expensive and struggles to perform well with high-resolution images. The A2 module (Area Attention) of YOLOv12 reduces expenses by dividing the feature maps, which are processed through simple reshaping, thereby eliminating the complexities associated with other attention techniques. This method not only provides the advantage of a wider receptive field but also faster processing, in such a way that the computational cost is reduced by almost 50%, making it possible to conduct the detection process efficiently and accurately on large-sized images [10].

2.4.2.2 R-ELAN
The feature aggregation is the core of every deep neural network's ability to share data. Earlier YOLO models depended
on ELAN to merge features; however, it caused delays for deep models in terms of gradient behavior and in how fast they converged due to its structure, as it blocks residual connections. R-ELAN in YOLOv12 addresses this problem by introducing residual shortcuts that directly connect the input to the output. It enhances the gradient flow, training stabilization, and convergence speed without significantly increasing computation [10].



2.5 Enhanced Model Framework 

The enhanced model framework shows the modification of the YOLOv12 architecture. It is designed to address YOLOv12’s noise vulnerability. It involves modifying the early part of the backbone by adding a dual-branch input stem. One branch utilizes the standard convolutional blocks from YOLOv12, which extract basic visual features. On the other hand, the second branch employs depthwise separable convolutions, which comprise depthwise convolutions, batch normalization, residual skip connection, and pointwise convolutions. After extracting the features of both branches, the model combines them using adaptive feature fusion. It learns how much each branch contributes in order to produce a single feature map.
After modifying the model, the dataset of underwater objects gathered from Roboflow, a platform widely used for computer vision dataset management and preparation, will be preprocessed and prepared for training.
After splitting and merging the dataset and the noise application, the enhanced model is trained. The enhanced model’s performance is compared to the baseline in terms of its effectiveness in noise robustness in underwater objects by applying combined noise and diffusion noise in the test set. The evaluation metrics: Precision (P), Recall (R), mAP@50, and mAP@50:95 will be utilized.
For the noise simulation in the train set, diffusion noise and salt-and-pepper noise are applied to the images in the train set to simulate the distortions that occur underwater. The same evaluation metrics will be used.

2.5.1 Standard Branch
The standard branch in the enhanced model is based on the original design of the baseline model. It consists of the first two convolutional blocks of YOLOv12. They focus on capturing features, such as color gradients, edges, and textures. These blocks serve as the path used for preserving the structural details of an image. The enhanced model allows clean and detailed feature extraction when these layers remain before proceeding further in the backbone.

2.5.2 Denoising Branch
The denoising branch does the feature extraction from the noisy image to describe its quality. It consists of two depthwise separable convolutions that handle the heavy image processing. The first layer of the depthwise separable convolution is the depthwise convolution. It serves as a filter that reduces the noise of an image while preserving the important details of the image simultaneously. The second layer is the pointwise convolution. It is the one that performs feature fusion and makes sure that more information remains consistent over all channels.

2.5.3 Adaptive Feature Fusion
The adaptive feature fusion fuses the outputs of the standard and denoising branches. It uses a learnable weighting procedure to do this. First, it stacks the feature maps of both branches in the channel dimension to form a combined single representation. A lightweight convolutional layer then estimates the fusion weights. After that, they are passed through a sigmoid activation function to produce values between 0 and 1. These normalized weights determine the contribution of each branch at every spatial location. 2.6.2 Precision (P)
Precision quantifies the percentage of underwater objects accurately identified among all the detections performed by the model. A high precision level signifies that the majority of the detections refer to real objects, hence reducing the number of false positives to a minimum.

2.6.3 Recall (R)
Recall shows how well the model is in classifying all underwater object instances. A high recall implies a small number of false negatives.

2.6.4 Mean Average Precision at IoU = 0.50 (mAP@50)
Mean Average Precision (mAP@50) is used to calculate the average precision across all classes in the dataset with a threshold of 0.50 IoU. It is the main metric in evaluating the detection performance of YOLO models.

2.6.5 Mean Average Precision at IoU = 0.50:0.95 (mAP@50:95)
mAP@50:95 is a stricter metric used to compute the average mAP values across the IoU thresholds that range from 0.50 to 0.95 with an interval of 0.05.
